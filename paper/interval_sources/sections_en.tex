\begin{abstract}
% P:abstract
Can two learners with similar measured specialization adapt differently to the same new learning condition? We study this question in small shared-representation networks whose formation histories and optimizer states can be controlled. Different observation and loss-weighting histories produce equivalent mean reconstruction measurements. Under a common intervention favoring the other target, the more precisely readable target switches within ten updates in 67.5\% of acquisition-formed checkpoints and 0\% of update-formed checkpoints. Resetting Adam reduces the between-route difference in reallocation to within a pre-specified equivalence margin. In an independent component-exchange experiment, swapping the second moment reverses which history is more susceptible. Further interventions separate its mean adaptive scaling from its coordinate-wise pattern: equalizing the mean scale reduces the difference, while exchanging the pattern at that common scale changes its sign. Fresh Adam and calibrated SGD reveal smaller delayed differences, while sustained common learning brings allocation endpoints close by 500 updates. Thus measured specialization can conceal a reversible difference in short-horizon adaptation, with second-moment structure acting as a causal modifier. The matched comparison extends to three Gaussian targets at two bottleneck capacities. A three-room sensor-data extension retains component effects but fails endpoint matching, delimiting the evidence for concealed susceptibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% P:intro1
A shared representation is an internal code used by several prediction tasks. With limited capacity, information useful for one task may remain easier to recover than information useful for another. We call this inequality in readout precision \emph{representational specialization}. Such biases are well established: feature complexity, learning order, and competition among information sources can change what a network represents \citep{lampinen2024biased,pezeshki2021gradient,huang2022modality}. We ask what a measured specialization tells us about the learner's response when learning conditions change.

% P:intro2
This question matters whenever a model continues learning after its performance has been evaluated. Two models may currently support similar predictions but require different amounts of learning to reallocate their shared features. Training history can affect later plasticity, the ability to learn from new information, through both model parameters and optimizer state \citep{achille2019critical,lyle2023plasticity,dohare2024plasticity}. The experimental challenge is to distinguish a difference in current measured ability from a difference in subsequent response, and then identify a persistent component that changes that response.

% P:intro3
We construct this comparison using two routes to specialization. One route observes a target more precisely while weighting reconstruction errors equally; the other balances precise observations while placing more weight on that target's error. Pilot calibration makes selected mean endpoint measurements equivalent within fixed margins. We then apply the same intervention favoring the other target. If those measurements adequately characterize adaptation under this intervention, the routes should respond similarly. If inherited optimizer state contributes to an otherwise hidden response difference, resetting or exchanging that state should change the contrast while leaving each recipient's model weights fixed at intervention onset.

% P:intro4
The results support the second explanation for a large early response difference. Adam's second moment, its running estimate of squared gradients, rescales updates separately for each parameter. Exchanging it reverses which formation route changes more. Equalizing its mean inverse scaling leaves a response difference, and exchanging its coordinate-wise pattern at a common mean scale changes the sign. These interventions identify how the organization of adaptive scaling modifies reallocation in the recipient network. Longer trajectories then show why the observation horizon matters: smaller route differences emerge even after the inherited Adam state is removed, and prolonged common learning brings the measured allocation endpoints close.

% P:intro5
Our empirical contribution has three connected parts: a comparison of adaptation after matching selected reconstruction measurements; component and scale/pattern interventions that change the short response difference; and a temporal comparison that separates this large inherited-state transient from smaller delayed differences. We use \emph{latent susceptibility} to name the response difference concealed by those endpoint measurements. Small artificial learners allow the paired histories, saved model states, and optimizer components needed for these tests. The matched comparison covers two- and three-target Gaussian systems. A natural sensor-data extension tests which parts survive real cross-target and temporal structure; complementary formation and maintenance experiments locate these results within the wider process of specialization.

\section{Relation to training history and optimizer memory}
\label{sec:related}
% P:related1
Similar measured performance can coexist with important differences between predictors, as studied under underspecification \citep{damour2022underspecification}. Representation-similarity measures examine another aspect of their endpoints \citep{kornblith2019similarity}. Our comparison concerns the response to further training: selected reconstruction measurements are matched before a common intervention. This connects the study to plasticity research, where early experience and training dynamics change later learning \citep{achille2019critical,jastrzebski2020breakeven,dohare2024plasticity}.

% P:related2
Optimizer-mediated history effects have direct precedents. \citet{lyle2023plasticity} relates plasticity to loss geometry and shows how unequal adaptation rates of Adam's moment estimates can destabilize training after task changes. \citet{asadi2023resetting} evaluates optimizer resets after changing reinforcement-learning objectives. \citet{ellis2024local} analyzes update-size transients after gradient-scale changes and introduces relative Adam timesteps. These results make inherited adaptive scaling a concrete explanation to test here. Our question is whether average scaling suffices to account for a route difference in \emph{representational reallocation}, or whether the coordinate-wise pattern also changes it.

% P:related3
Direct state transplantation has also been used to study hidden training effects. In a recent preprint, \citet{xu2026optimizer} identifies a first-moment carrier of subliminal transfer: an intervention invisible in immediate outputs produces differences during subsequent training. \citet{sevetlidis2026trainingmemory} organizes historical carriers and proposes state-based causal measurements. The present contribution is a specific empirical decomposition of shared-representation adaptation. It combines matched reconstruction measurements, second-moment scale/pattern interventions, and a common continuation under inherited Adam, fresh Adam, and SGD. Table~\ref{tab:prior} summarizes the closest comparisons.

These studies establish why training state matters, while the measured outcomes differ. A change in specialization under a ten-update pulse is neither an optimization-performance improvement nor a measure of accumulated trait transfer. The interval for the change in response contrast under first-moment exchange lies within the pre-specified equivalence margin at this horizon. The longer trajectories test the duration of reallocation effects under a common opposing task. Transfer of learned attributes to a distinct downstream task remains an open comparison.

\section{A controlled comparison of specialization and adaptation}
\label{sec:approach}
\subsection{Two formation routes through one shared bottleneck}
% P:design1
The central learner reconstructs two three-dimensional signals, $A$ and $B$, from noisy observations. We generate standard Gaussian $A$ and independent Gaussian $\epsilon$, and set $B=\rho A+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\epsilon$ with $\rho=0.35$. A seed-specific orthogonal transformation mixes the observations. A $6\to32\to2$ encoder, with tanh after both layers, maps them to a two-number code shared by two linear reconstruction heads. Training targets are the noise-free signals. This bottleneck makes the allocation of readable information observable.

% P:design2
We manipulate information quality and error weighting separately. \emph{Acquisition precision} changes target-specific input noise before mixing; the high- and low-precision standard deviations are 0.05 and 1.20. Dividing each noisy channel by $\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}$ normalizes its variance. \emph{Update allocation} changes the reconstruction-loss weights:
\begin{equation}
L_t=w_{A,t}\ell_{A,t}+w_{B,t}\ell_{B,t},\qquad w_{A,t}+w_{B,t}=1.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Equal weights give the errors equal explicit importance; their gradients can still differ. Observation quality therefore enters learning through a different intervention from changing loss weights. Related work studies active acquisition and task weighting \citep{shim2018joint,chen2022active,chen2018gradnorm,yu2020gradient}; the present design crosses these controls in one shared representation.

% P:design3
After 100 balanced initialization updates, a nine-condition acquisition-by-weighting comparison runs for 500 updates to test which target each control favors. Balanced acquisition allocates exactly 250 high-precision updates to each target. For the matched comparison, the acquisition route observes the favored target precisely with equal loss weights; the update route balances acquisition and assigns the favored target weight 0.80. Route strengths are calibrated on pilot seeds and then fixed for confirmation. Full formation and redirection controls appear in Appendix~\ref{app:formation}.

\subsection{Common measurements and paired inference}
% P:design4
To measure readable information, we freeze the encoder, fit fresh linear readouts on unused probe data, and evaluate on separate held-out data. Both targets receive common high-precision inputs. Let $D_A,D_B$ be the variance-normalized mean squared errors. The allocation index
\begin{equation}
s=\frac{D_B-D_A}{D_A+D_B}
\label{eq:s}
\end{equation}
is positive when $A$ is more precisely readable. We align its sign to the originally favored target. A decrease then measures reallocation toward the other target, and crossing zero changes which target is more readable. We also retain both errors, their sum, and allocation under a common low-precision probe. Thus relative advantage is evaluated alongside absolute reconstruction quality.

% P:design5
Each seed supplies a common ancestor and paired input/noise sequences. Conditions branch with the same initial model and, unless manipulated, optimizer state. Pilot and confirmatory seeds are disjoint; each principal comparison uses 40 confirmatory seeds. Mirrored target labels are averaged within seed for inference. We report 95\% seed-bootstrap intervals. Endpoint equivalence requires the interval for the mean paired difference to lie inside each pre-specified margin; it is not an assertion that every pair has identical measurements. All confirmatory seeds are retained. Model parameters and representation geometry may differ after formation.

% P:design7
Before confirmatory runs, each plan fixes the largest acceptable mean differences in allocation-index or normalized-error units. Appendix~\ref{app:optimizer} lists these matching tolerances and response margins, together with the separate pilot stages.

% P:design6
The core evidence comprises the endpoint-matching experiment (IV-D), independent component exchanges and a within-route control (IV-E), and a prospective scale/pattern and time-course follow-up on 40 further seeds. Supporting experiments address formation (IV-B), redirection (IV-A/C), maintenance (II/III), and three-target formation (V-A). The three-target adaptation extension (V-B) and a natural sensor-data comparison (V-C) test the scope of the component effects with new seeds. Appendix~\ref{app:optimizer} gives the exact interventions and full estimates, and Appendix~\ref{app:status} separates pre-specified tests, exploratory analyses, and failed candidates.

\section{Matched specialization conceals a short-horizon response difference}
\label{sec:latent}
% P:response0
Both controls can select specialization: under equal loss weights, precise acquisition of $A$ or $B$ produces mean allocation indices of 0.3385 and $-0.3338$ (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}a), while sufficiently strong opposing update weights can overcome this acquisition advantage.

% P:response1
The two formation routes reach similar measured specializations. All five endpoint criteria pass: aligned $s$, target and non-target errors, total error, and low-precision aligned $s$. The mean aligned-$s$ difference is $-0.0064$ ($[-0.0098,-0.0032]$), inside $\pm0.02$. Mean integrated responses over 200 neutral continuation updates, with balanced observation and equal weights, are also equivalent with either inherited or fresh Adam. Matching therefore covers selected static measurements and one neutral-continuation summary, while leaving response to an opposing condition to be tested.

% P:response2
Each formed learner is copied into three ten-update opposing interventions. An acquisition-only pulse observes the previously less readable target precisely with equal loss weights. An update-only pulse balances acquisition and gives that target weight 0.80. A combined pulse observes it precisely and assigns it weight 0.85. Each pulse is tested with inherited or freshly initialized Adam, keeping the recipient model weights unchanged at the branch point. The response is the decrease in aligned $s$; the route contrast is acquisition-formed minus update-formed decrease.

% P:response3
With inherited Adam, the acquisition-formed route moves further under every pulse (Table~\ref{tab:pulses}). Under the combined pulse, 67.5\% of its checkpoints cross zero within ten updates, compared with 0\% of the update-formed checkpoints (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}b). The continuous route difference is 0.1951. Resetting Adam reduces it to 0.0103, within the pre-specified $\pm0.02$ margin. The other pulses show the same reduction. Thus inherited optimizer state strongly modifies a response difference that selected endpoint measurements and neutral continuation did not reveal.

% P:response4
This result applies to the two-target network and the ten-update pulses. A separate linear measure of representation similarity (CKA) averages 0.927, and the model parameters are not identical. Resetting Adam leaves those model differences intact. The small reset contrast therefore identifies a limited response equivalence at this horizon, rather than identical representations. The zero-crossing rates likewise describe the brief pulse; Section~\ref{sec:time} examines longer common learning.

% P:endpointprediction
An exploratory check across the three existing 40-seed cohorts asks whether residual endpoint differences predict the response contrast within seeds. Linear models using target error, other-target error, and low-precision allocation provide little improvement over predicting the training-fold mean. This supports the limited predictive value of these measured endpoint features; Appendix~\ref{app:extension} reports the held-out errors and conditional estimates.

\section{Second-moment structure causally modifies reallocation}
\label{sec:mechanism}
\subsection{Component exchange reverses the route contrast}
% P:mechanism1
Adam retains a signed-gradient average $m$ and a squared-gradient average $v$ \citep{kingma2015adam}. The latter sets a separate adaptive scale for each parameter. To test these components directly, we exchange $m$ or $v$ between route endpoints while holding each recipient's weights and optimizer step count fixed. Corresponding parameter coordinates are available because both routes descend from the same ancestor.

% P:mechanism2
In an independent 40-seed comparison, exchanging $m$ leaves the mean response contrast nearly unchanged, with the change inside the pre-specified margin. Exchanging $v$ changes the route contrast from $+0.1968$ to $-0.1265$ (Figure~\ref{fig:moments}a). Both estimates' intervals exclude zero in opposite directions (Appendix~\ref{app:optimizer}). The recipient's second-moment state therefore changes which route is more susceptible. This intervention identifies a causal modifier acting together with the recipient weights and subsequent gradients; it measures the combined ten-update response.

\subsection{Average scaling accounts for only part of the short response difference}
% P:mechanism3
The exchange raises a more specific question: does one route simply receive a larger average adaptive scale? In a prospective follow-up on 40 new seeds, the inherited-state contrast is 0.1907, reproducing the earlier 0.1951 and 0.1968 estimates. We define $\bar a_v$ as the mean of Adam's bias-corrected inverse-second-moment scaling terms across parameters. This quantity describes the scaling supplied by $v$; an actual update also uses the first moment and current gradient.

% P:mechanism4
Equalizing $\bar a_v$ while preserving each route's coordinate pattern reduces the response difference from 0.1907 to 0.0875. Replacing the pattern by a scalar value gives 0.1251; combining scalarization with a common scale gives 0.0395 (Figure~\ref{fig:timecourse}a). Exchanging the other route's pattern while holding $\bar a_v$ common changes the difference to $-0.0110$. Average scaling thus accounts for part of the response contrast, while coordinate pattern also changes the recipient's response at that common mean. This comparison equalizes a mean inverse-scaling quantity, not the actual update norms throughout the pulse. The post-hoc factorial recombination remains exploratory in Appendix~\ref{app:optimizer}.

\subsection{The effect also occurs within one formation-route category}
% P:mechanism5
A within-route control holds acquisition, mini-batches, update count, and mean target loss weight fixed. One schedule uses weight 0.80 throughout 500 updates; the other uses 1.00 and 0.60 for 250 updates each in randomized order. The endpoint criteria again pass. The constant schedule then moves further under the common pulse by 0.0324 ($[0.0294,0.0354]$). Exchanging $v$ reverses the contrast to $-0.0141$, while resetting Adam reduces it to 0.0046, inside $\pm0.01$ (Figure~\ref{fig:moments}b). Different acquisition-versus-update labels are therefore unnecessary for this response pattern: equal average loss weights can conceal different optimizer states and later responses. Variability and realized sequence change jointly in this control.

\subsection{Realized parameter movement links scaling to response}
% P:mechanism6
How does adaptive scaling relate to movement during the pulse? An exploratory analysis of the fixed follow-up trajectories measures cumulative path length by summing the distance moved by each update (its Euclidean norm). Over ten updates with inherited Adam, the acquisition-to-update route ratio is 0.987 ($[0.978,0.996]$) for cumulative gradient norm and 1.872 ($[1.844,1.902]$) for parameter-path length. Thus a gradient-norm ratio close to one accompanies substantially greater parameter movement in the acquisition-formed route.

% P:mechanism7
Across the same 40 seeds, the between-route path-length difference correlates 0.806 with the reallocation-response difference. Holding out each seed in turn, single-variable linear models reduce mean squared error by 28\% using the pre-intervention $\bar a_v$ ratio and 62\% using the realized path-length difference, relative to predicting the mean response difference of the other 39 seeds. Realized movement thus provides a closer reconstruction of the response difference in this exploratory comparison.

% P:mechanism8
A movement-controlled intervention on the 40 saved follow-up seed pairs tests whether unequal global path lengths are sufficient to explain the contrast. At every update, it rescales the four proposed vectors (two routes, with own or exchanged $v$) to their common geometric-mean norm while preserving each direction. The route contrast is then 0.0303 with own $v$ and 0.0072 with exchanged $v$; their paired difference is 0.0231. Thus $v$ still alters reallocation under equal global movement. This controlled learning rule changes the trajectories and does not estimate a natural-Adam mediation fraction (Appendix~\ref{app:extension}).

% P:mechanism9
Layer-restricted exchanges locate a substantial part of this effect in the encoder. Starting from the original contrast 0.1907, exchanging only first-layer $v$ leaves 0.0115, exchanging only second-layer $v$ leaves 0.0611, and exchanging both heads' $v$ leaves 0.1911. These interventions retain the recipient weights and all other optimizer components. Their non-additive effects localize effective interventions, rather than assigning independent shares of the full contrast.

\subsection{Which parts of the comparison transfer across settings?}
% P:generalization
A fixed-protocol replication uses 32 new paired seeds at each of four settings: the baseline, hidden width 64, ReLU in place of tanh, and independent targets. All four satisfy the five endpoint tolerances. Each shows a positive inherited-state contrast, reversal under $v$ exchange, and an exchange effect at common $\bar a_v$. Reset equivalence is more conditional: at width 64 the reset contrast is 0.0249, with its interval above 0.02. The replicated component effect therefore transfers more broadly than the claim that resetting Adam makes the ten-update responses equivalent. Appendix~\ref{app:extension} gives every condition, including the failed reset criterion.

% P:generalizationbeta
Varying $\beta_2$ and formation duration further bounds this transfer. Larger $\beta_2$ produces larger inherited-state contrasts in both tested duration groups, but $v$ exchange does not reverse every contrast. Common-scale exchange effects remain positive throughout the six-setting grid. Thus changing second-moment dynamics alters the component effect, while reversal and reset equivalence depend on the training regime.

\subsection{Three targets preserve the matched comparison; natural data delimit it}
% P:vbmain
Does the component effect depend on having only one alternative target? We form acquisition- and update-driven histories for three symmetric Gaussian targets and test all six ordered changes of favored target. Eight pilot seeds calibrate endpoint measurements without observing adaptation; 40 new seeds test bottleneck dimensions two and three. The response is the decrease in the pairwise allocation index for the original and destination targets, retaining all three reconstruction errors. Both capacities meet all eight mean endpoint criteria. Exchanging $v$ reverses the ten-update route contrast, and exchanging its pattern at a common $\bar a_v$ changes the contrast at both capacities (Table~\ref{tab:bridges}). Reset equivalence holds at dimension two; at dimension three its interval crosses the $+0.02$ boundary and remains unresolved (Appendix~\ref{app:threeadapt}).

% P:vcmain
We then replace Gaussian targets with recorded temperature and humidity in a kitchen, living room, and laundry room from one house \citep{candanedo2017data,candanedo2017dataset}. Noise controls observation precision; shared learning reconstructs the recorded measurements. Chronologically separated blocks distinguish training, pilot readout/evaluation, and confirmation readout/evaluation. Room-specific calibration on eight pilot seeds fails to match endpoints, and confirmation on 40 new seeds also fails at both capacities. Nevertheless, with recipient weights fixed at exchange, changing $v$ changes the response contrast, including at a common mean inverse scale. The swapped contrast is negative at dimension two but unresolved around zero at dimension three. This establishes a component effect on natural sensor measurements, while a matched-endpoint demonstration on natural data remains open (Appendix~\ref{app:natural}).

\section{Optimizer condition changes when the route difference appears}
\label{sec:time}
% P:time1
A small difference immediately after reset could still grow during continued learning. We therefore extend the opposing intervention to 500 updates under inherited Adam, fresh Adam, or momentum-free SGD. SGD's common learning rate is calibrated within each seed and target direction using first-batch inherited-Adam update norms and gradient norms, then held fixed; allocation responses are not used for calibration. All three continuations start from the Adam-formed route endpoints.

% P:time2
Inherited Adam produces its largest route differences after 10--20 updates. Fresh Adam gives 0.0096 at update 10 but 0.0274 at update 50; calibrated SGD gives 0.0123 at update 10, 0.0433 at update 50, and 0.0309 at update 100 (Figure~\ref{fig:timecourse}b). The update-50 intervals for fresh Adam and SGD lie above 0.02. That margin was fixed for the ten-update test and is only a reference for later points. The observations show delayed route differences after inherited Adam state has been removed, implicating differences retained on the model side without identifying their specific parameter or geometric source.

% P:time3
The route contrasts shrink toward zero by update 500. Under inherited Adam, the endpoint allocation difference is $-0.00045$ ($[-0.00146,0.00057]$). This endpoint difference is distinct from the difference in change since formation, because the starting measurements were close rather than identical. The inherited-minus-fresh response contrast also changes sign by update 50, so the inherited trajectory is not described by adding a fixed nonnegative fast component to the fresh-Adam trajectory. Within this common opposing condition, optimizer state changes the timing and magnitude of reallocation; the large early difference does not imply a persistent allocation gap.

\section{Implications and limits of the comparison}
\label{sec:scope}
% P:scope1
The result gives a concrete way to audit continued learning. Measure current specialization, apply the same future learning condition, and test which persistent components alter the response. In this construction, equivalent mean reconstruction measurements coexist with a large early contrast that can be reversed by exchanging $v$. Mean adaptive scale explains only part of that contrast. The equal-mean loss-weight control further shows that average task emphasis can conceal different retained states and responses. Together, these comparisons connect optimizer memory to the reallocation of information within a shared representation.

% P:scope2
A concrete application is evaluating a checkpoint before resuming training on a changed task. The comparison suggests testing a common continuation with optimizer state inherited and reset, alongside current readout quality. In the present system these choices change the timing and magnitude of adaptation. Whether the same diagnostic improves decisions in fine-tuning requires validation on the target task, including its eventual performance.

% P:scope3
Complementary experiments distinguish susceptibility from formation and maintenance. Current acquisition can redirect an already formed specialization under equal subsequent loss weights over 1,000 updates (Appendix~\ref{app:formation}). Symmetrizing acquisition and updating reduces a formed bias, while designed state-following feedback can support long-lived regimes (Appendix~\ref{app:maintenance}). A symmetric three-target construction reproduces acquisition-selected formation at two bottleneck dimensions after 600 updates (Appendix~\ref{app:three}). These controls establish that formation, current support, and response require different interventions. Three-target adaptation now supplies a separate test of susceptibility (Appendix~\ref{app:threeadapt}).

% P:scope4
The matched adaptation evidence covers two or three Gaussian targets, small bottleneck networks, externally specified observation and loss weights, and finite horizons. The sensor extension covers one building and fixed chronological blocks; its seed intervals measure training/noise variability conditional on those records. Its unmatched endpoints prevent attributing the unmodified route contrast to a difference concealed by equivalent measurements. Establishing that comparison on natural data requires independently calibrated matching across richer histories and validation in other buildings or tasks. Coordinate exchange remains defined by common parameter ancestry, and equal global update norms still allow different directions and layerwise movement.

\section{Conclusion}
% P:conclusion
Similar measured specialization can conceal latent susceptibility to further learning in the studied two- and three-target Gaussian systems. Component exchange identifies inherited Adam second moments as a causal modifier of early reallocation, with effects beyond mean adaptive scale and, in the two-target control, beyond global update norm. The natural sensor extension retains component effects but fails endpoint matching, separating transfer of the intervention effect from transfer of the concealed-response claim. Reset equivalence and sign reversal depend on capacity, learning conditions, and horizon. Current readout quality, persistent training state, and subsequent learning conditions jointly determine what an adaptation comparison reveals.
